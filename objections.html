A frequent counter-argument is that capital owners simply won't need human consumers at all: “If machines can do all the work, robots will just build datacenters, fusion power grids, and orbital rockets for the wealthy. Capital can circulate machine-to-machine within an elite loop without needing ordinary people to buy products.”

This sounds plausible until you look at how industrial economies actually function. First, capital goods are intermediate goods—they exist to produce final goods. A supply chain where machines only build machines to build more machines is an engine running in neutral with no terminal purpose.

Second, advanced technology requires massive aggregate volume. An extreme ultraviolet lithography machine or a frontier chip fab requires hundreds of thousands of specialized parts from thousands of suppliers. You cannot amortize the trillions of dollars needed for continuous hardware development on the purchasing power of a few hundred wealthy families. If you shrink the customer base to a country club, the industrial division of labor physically atrophies.

Most importantly, corporations are legal entities chartered by sovereign democratic states, not alien spacecraft hovering in orbit. They rely on public power grids, municipal water, contract courts, and physical security. The idea that a handful of corporate boards can run an insular high-tech paradise inside a sovereign nation while telling 150 million voting citizens to go away ignores the basic reality of state sovereignty.

The Intermediate Capital Fallacy: Machines building machines sounds self-contained, but capital equipment is not final wealth. An AI datacenter or an automated excavator has zero terminal value on its own; it only creates value if its output is consumed. Without a broad consumer base to clear final goods, an insular elite loop quickly collapses under the fixed costs of maintaining global supply chains.

A related objection concedes that people won't be allowed to starve, but warns of a different trap: “Elites won't share real wealth; they'll just pacify the population with bare-minimum in-kind rations—standardized modular housing, synthetic food, and virtual reality—while keeping the money, capital, and political control for themselves.”

This overlooks the basic logistics of distribution. Managing a population on direct in-kind handouts recreates the Soviet calculation problem. Who decides who gets the apartment with a view versus the basement? Who decides who gets craft tools, sports gear, or musical instruments? Centralized rationing boards are corrupt, inefficient, and despised by the public.

To give people any choice over their daily lives, you have to hand them accounting tokens. And the moment you give citizens fungible tokens to command automated surplus, you have created a universal cash dividend.

Furthermore, businesses are not a single, unified cabal. Companies that make housing, home appliances, food, and digital entertainment compete against each other for customer dollars. They actively want consumers to have liquid purchasing power so they can sell to them. The alternative—trying to lock the public into non-transferable company scrip—collapses into black markets almost immediately. A universal dividend isn't a top-down ration; it is the open-market coordination mechanism for an automated economy.
